The river had been the town's reason for existing long before anyone thought to write the fact down. Barges came up from the coast twice a week in the dry season, carrying salt, iron stock, and bolts of cloth, and went back down loaded with timber and wool. The wharf masters kept their ledgers in a script so cramped that only they could read it, which was, as far as anyone could tell, the point. A town that lives on a river learns the river's moods the way a farmer learns weather: not as information but as habit, a thing felt in the knees before it is understood in the head.

The bridge changed all of that, though not in the way its builders expected. They had argued for it as a convenience, a way to bring the eastern farms into the market square without the long detour through the ford at Miller's Bend. What it actually did was reorganize the town's sense of itself. Within a generation the warehouses had migrated to the eastern bank, where land was cheap and the new road ran straight toward the railhead. The old wharf district kept its name and lost its purpose, and the wharf masters' ledgers went into a cabinet in the back room of the customs house, where they stayed until the flood of the following spring made the question of preserving them moot.

Historians who study small towns like this one often complain that the record is thin precisely where it matters most. The minutes of the town council survive in unbroken sequence for eighty years, recording in exhausting detail the disputes over grazing rights and the annual argument about the schoolmaster's salary. But the decision that actually shaped the town, the vote to site the bridge at the narrows rather than at the ford, occupies a single line, undated, in a hand that matches none of the clerks known to have served in that decade. Everything important happened between the lines, in conversations no one thought worth recording because everyone involved already knew what had been said.

This is a general problem, not a local one. The evidence that survives is the evidence that was cheap to keep, and the evidence that was cheap to keep is rarely the evidence that would answer the questions later generations think to ask. A merchant's daybook survives because paper was reused for wrapping; a treaty survives because seven copies were made and sworn; but the reasoning behind the treaty, the weighing of harms and advantages that consumed a winter of argument, survives only if someone was indiscreet enough to put it in a letter and lucky enough to have the letter kept.

The town's economy after the railway arrived is a study in adaptation under constraint. Wool no longer paid, because the mills in the valley towns could buy directly from the plateau stations at prices no local grower could match. Timber still paid, but only just, and only for the straight-grained lengths the mine works wanted for shoring. What the town discovered, more or less by accident, was that its position at the narrows made it a natural place to break cargo, to move goods from barge to railcar, and that the skills of the old wharf trade, the knowledge of loads and balances and the ways rope behaves in wet weather, transferred almost without modification to the new work.

By the century's end the town had become something its founders would not have recognized and might not have approved of: a junction, a place things passed through rather than a place things came from. The census takers counted more railway employees than farmers. The school taught bookkeeping and telegraphy. And the river, which had made the town and then been made irrelevant to it, ran on under the bridge as it always had, high in the spring and low in the autumn, indifferent to the ledgers, the minutes, the letters, and every other partial record of the people who had lived beside it and called it theirs.

What remains, for anyone patient enough to look, is a kind of palimpsest. The street plan still bends toward a wharf that no longer exists. The oldest houses still turn their best rooms toward the water. The names persist: Rope Walk, Tally Lane, the Ferry Steps that now descend to a car park. A town, like any long-lived institution, is an argument its past keeps having with its present, and the terms of the argument are set by what happened to survive.
